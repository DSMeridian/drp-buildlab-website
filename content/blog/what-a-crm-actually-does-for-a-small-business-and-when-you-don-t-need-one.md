Ask ten small business owners what a CRM is and you will get eleven answers. A fancy address book. An expensive spreadsheet. Something the sales team of a big company uses.

The honest answer is simpler: a CRM is a shared memory. It remembers who you talked to, what you promised and what happens next, so that it does not all live in one person's head.

> A CRM does not win you customers. It stops you from forgetting the ones you already have.

## Signs you have outgrown the spreadsheet

If three of these sound familiar, your customers deserve better than a tab called 'Sheet2':

- Two people keep their own list of the same customers.
- Follow-ups depend on someone remembering to 'call them next week'.
- You cannot answer 'how many quotes are still open?' without asking around.
- A customer asks for a copy of an invoice and it takes three people and two inboxes.
- When someone goes on holiday, their customers go quiet.

## What a good CRM does for a small business

Five things, in plain words:

1. **One place per customer.** Contact details, emails, quotes, invoices and notes together.
2. **A history you can read in thirty seconds.** Before a call you see what was said last time.
3. **Follow-ups that remind you.** You set a date once, and the system nudges you instead of your memory.
4. **A simple pipeline.** Stages such as new request, quote sent, accepted and done, so you can see where work gets stuck.
5. **Connections to the tools you already use.** Website forms, email, calendar, invoicing and payments, so nobody retypes the same data.

## A small example

Marc runs a three-person tiling company. (Marc is made up, his problem is not.) Requests arrive through the website form, WhatsApp and the phone. Quotes live in Word documents with names like quote_final_v3_FINAL.docx.

With a CRM, every request becomes one card. A website request appears automatically, the quote is attached, a reminder is set for three days later and the status moves to 'accepted' when the customer says yes. An accepted quote can start an invoice draft. What used to be a weekly hunt through messages becomes a short look at one screen.

## When you don't need a CRM

Be honest with yourself. You can skip it if:

- You have a handful of repeat customers and know them all by heart.
- Only one person ever talks to customers.
- You will not keep it up to date. A CRM with empty fields is worse than a spreadsheet.

Start with a plain spreadsheet that has five columns: name, contact details, last contact, next step and the date of that next step. If you keep that tidy for a month, you know what you need.

## Buy or build?

Ready-made tools are cheap to start with and fine for standard sales processes. They get awkward when your process is not standard: you quote by area, you need an approval step, or you have to connect to a supplier system. Then you spend more time bending the tool than using it.

A custom CRM is built around your process. It holds only the fields and steps you actually use and connects directly to your website, invoicing and payments. The price is a higher upfront cost and someone who maintains it. Whether that is worth it depends on how much of your day goes into admin that a system could do.

## Start in one week

1. **Monday:** write down your real process in five steps, from first request to paid invoice.
2. **Tuesday:** list what you want to see at a glance, such as open quotes and overdue follow-ups.
3. **Wednesday:** move your customers into one list and remove duplicates.
4. **Thursday:** make one rule: every contact gets a next step and a date.
5. **Friday:** review for ten minutes. Decide whether a spreadsheet is enough or whether you need a tool.

## An honest note

No tool fixes a process nobody follows. A CRM only works if the habit comes first: write it down, set the next step, look at it once a week. The software just makes that habit easier.
