Meet the best intern you have ever had. Starts at six in the morning. Never complains. Has read most of the internet. Writes a decent email in four seconds.

There are three small problems. The intern is very sure of itself, even when it is wrong. It does not know what 'confidential' means. And it will happily work with whatever you hand over, without asking who else might see it.

Congratulations, you have an AI tool. And your team is probably already using it.

> Treat AI like a brilliant intern. Not like a vault.

## What the intern is great at

First drafts. Summaries. Brainstorming ten titles when you have none. Turning your messy notes into something a human can read. For this kind of work it is fast, tireless and surprisingly good.

What it is not good at: knowing your rules, your clients, or when to say 'I'm not sure'. It sounds equally confident when it is right and when it is making things up. That is the thing to remember.

## Rule one: do not hand over the keys

You would not give an intern on day one the company bank card, the passwords and the client list. Same here.

Noor works in accounting. (Noor is made up, the situation is not.) A supplier sends a long email about a disputed invoice. Noor pastes the whole thing into a free chatbot to get 'a polite reply'. The reply is excellent. The email also contained the supplier's name, bank details and an invoice number, which are now in a place Noor no longer controls.

Nothing dramatic happens. But it did not have to happen at all. The simple test: do not paste anything you would not email to a stranger.

## Rule two: check the work

The intern's best skill is sounding right. So check what matters: names, numbers, dates and promises.

This is not only about embarrassment. In 2024 a tribunal in Canada held Air Canada responsible for what the chatbot on its own website had told a customer about a refund. If your AI says it, it is your company saying it.

## Rule three: start with the boring jobs

Nobody gives a new intern the biggest client in week one. Let the AI prove itself on low-stakes work first: summarising your own meeting notes, drafting a first version of a blog post, suggesting subject lines. When the results are reliable, give it bigger jobs, with a human still reading the output.

## Rule four: tell people when the intern is talking

If a customer chats with an AI on your website, say so in the first sentence. People forgive a bot for not knowing something. They do not forgive being tricked. (We wrote a whole article about that, because it matters.)

## Rule five: give the intern a proper desk

Right now, your team probably uses AI the way people use a stray pen: whichever tab is open, whichever account they signed up for. A proper desk looks different:

- A short list of approved tools, on business plans, with the data settings checked.
- Clear rules about what never goes in.
- Where it makes sense, AI connected to your own systems, such as your CRM or documents, with access rules and a log of what it did, and with personal data removed before anything leaves.

That last step is more work than opening a browser tab. It is also the difference between hoping nothing goes wrong and knowing where the data goes.

## The version for the fridge

If you remember only five sentences:

1. No passwords, no personal data, no contracts.
2. Check names, numbers and promises.
3. Start with low-risk tasks.
4. Say it when AI is talking.
5. If you slip, say so right away. Nobody gets in trouble for being quick.

## A fair note

To be fair to the intern: good AI tools on a business plan do not gossip. Reputable providers usually promise that your input is not used for training and give you controls. But 'usually' is not 'always', and the settings differ per tool, so check yours.

There is also a rule on the books. The EU AI Act expects organisations that use AI to make sure their people understand it well enough for their job. A short team session with real examples is a good start. Ask your adviser what applies to you.

*Noor does not exist. The intern is real, and starts at six.*
